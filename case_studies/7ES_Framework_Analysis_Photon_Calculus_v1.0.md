## DETAILED ANALYSIS BY ELEMENT

### Element 1: INPUT (I) — 4 Subsystems Identified

The photon's input space comprises the conditions and parameters that bring a photon into existence and determine its initial state. Four functionally distinct input subsystems were identified, each utilizing different physical mechanisms and responding to different stimulus categories.

**I.1 — Source Process Energy**
The energy input that determines the photon's frequency (and therefore its fundamental character) via E = hν. Source processes are physically diverse: atomic electron transitions (discrete spectral lines), charged particle acceleration (bremsstrahlung, synchrotron radiation), particle-antiparticle annihilation (e⁺e⁻ → γγ), nuclear transitions (gamma rays), and thermal radiation (blackbody spectrum). Each mechanism constitutes a different physical pathway for photon creation. The source process encodes energy information into the photon's frequency — this is an irreversible energy-to-information transformation at the point of creation.

**I.2 — Polarization State**
The polarization input specifying the photon's spin-1 helicity state. Polarization is determined by the geometry and symmetry of the emission process (antenna orientation, atomic orbital angular momentum, crystal structure in nonlinear optics). This is informationally distinct from energy input: two photons can share identical frequency but carry orthogonal polarization states. Polarization represents a two-dimensional complex state space (|R⟩, |L⟩ or equivalently |H⟩, |V⟩) — a genuine quantum degree of freedom independent of energy.

**I.3 — Propagation Direction / Momentum**
The wavevector k⃗ determining the photon's direction of travel, with magnitude |k| = ω/c. Direction is constrained by the source geometry and conservation of momentum at the emission vertex. For stimulated emission (lasers), direction is further constrained by the stimulating field. This input is spatially vectorial, contrasting with the scalar nature of energy input (I.1) and the internal state nature of polarization input (I.2).

**I.4 — Quantum Identity Parameters**
The fixed quantum numbers defining photon identity: spin s = 1, mass m = 0, electric charge q = 0, and gauge boson status under U(1)_EM. These parameters are not variable per photon instance but constitute the "architectural specification" input — the template from which every photon is instantiated. They enter from the structure of quantum electrodynamics itself (the QED Lagrangian).

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These four inputs are genuine subsystems rather than artificial subdivisions. They utilize different physical mechanisms (thermodynamic vs. geometric vs. quantum-algebraic), respond to different aspects of the source event, and are independently variable. A photon's energy can change without altering its polarization (Doppler shift); its polarization can rotate without changing energy (Faraday rotation); its direction can change without changing energy or polarization (gravitational lensing at certain limits). The quantum identity parameters (I.4) are invariant across all photon instances, distinguishing them from the other three subsystems which are instance-specific.

### Element 2: OUTPUT (O) — 4 Subsystems Identified

The photon's output space encompasses the effects, products, and state changes it delivers to external systems upon interaction. Four distinct output channels were identified.

**O.1 — Energy Delivery**
Transfer of quantized energy E = hν to an absorbing system. This output drives photoelectric emission, photochemical reactions, thermal heating, and photosynthetic energy capture. The energy is delivered as a single quantum — the photon is annihilated in the process of complete absorption. This all-or-nothing character distinguishes quantum energy delivery from classical wave energy transfer.

**O.2 — Momentum Transfer**
Delivery of linear momentum p = ℏk to matter, manifesting as radiation pressure, photon recoil, and optical trapping forces. Momentum transfer is the mechanism behind solar sails, laser cooling of atoms, and the Compton effect. This output channel operates through a different physical mechanism than energy delivery (O.1) — momentum transfer can occur in scattering events where the photon is not absorbed, only redirected.

**O.3 — Information Output**
Transmission of encoded information via polarization state, phase, coherence properties, temporal modulation, and spectral content. This output channel is the basis of all optical communication, astronomical spectroscopy (conveying information about distant source composition, temperature, velocity), and quantum information protocols (quantum key distribution, teleportation channels). Information output is functionally independent of energy output — the same energy can carry different information content depending on modulation.

**O.4 — Field Effects**
Contribution to the electromagnetic field configuration experienced by other charged particles. A photon mediates the electromagnetic force between charges (virtual photon exchange in QED). Even as a real propagating photon, it modifies the local field environment. This output is continuous and nonlocal during propagation, contrasting with the localized, discrete character of absorption-based outputs (O.1, O.2).

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These outputs target different recipient systems (absorber, scatterer, detector, field), employ different transfer mechanisms (annihilation vs. scattering vs. encoding vs. field superposition), and produce functionally independent effects. Momentum transfer (O.2) can occur without energy absorption (elastic scattering). Information transfer (O.3) can be decoded without absorbing the photon (quantum nondemolition measurement, in principle). Field effects (O.4) operate during propagation, before any absorption event.

### Element 6: INTERFACE (N) — 5 Subsystems Identified

The Interface element proved to be among the most structurally rich in this analysis. Five distinct boundary-crossing mechanisms were identified, each mediating a qualitatively different regime transition. Close examination reveals that each interface involves characteristic energy-information transformations.

**N.1 — Emission Interface (Matter → Radiation)**
The boundary crossing where energy bound in matter (atomic excitation, kinetic energy of charges, rest mass of annihilating pairs) transforms into freely propagating radiation. This interface mediates the transition from localized, massive, charged-particle states to delocalized, massless, neutral photon states.

*Energy flow:* Energy transforms from potential/kinetic forms in matter to electromagnetic radiation energy (E = hν). The transformation is governed by selection rules (ΔL = ±1 for electric dipole transitions) that constrain which transitions produce photons.

*Information flow:* Information about the source system's quantum state is encoded into the photon's frequency, polarization, and emission timing. Spectral line frequencies encode atomic energy level structure; polarization encodes transition geometry; coherence properties encode source statistics (thermal vs. stimulated). This is an information-writing operation.

**N.2 — Absorption Interface (Radiation → Matter)**
The boundary crossing where photon energy and information are transferred to a material absorber. This is the inverse of N.1 but involves distinct physics: the photon is annihilated and its quantum numbers must be accommodated by the absorber's available transitions.

*Energy flow:* Electromagnetic energy transforms into electronic excitation, thermal energy, or photochemical energy in the absorber. The photoelectric effect, photosynthetic energy capture, and retinal photoisomerization (vision) are all instances of this interface.

*Information flow:* Information encoded in the photon (frequency, polarization, phase, arrival time) is decoded by the absorber. Spectroscopic detection, optical communication reception, and quantum state measurement all occur at this interface. This is an information-reading operation.

**N.3 — Scattering Interface (Radiation ↔ Matter, Partial)**
The boundary crossing where the photon interacts with matter without full absorption, changing direction, energy, and/or polarization. Mechanisms include Compton scattering (inelastic, energy changes), Thomson scattering (elastic, direction changes), Rayleigh scattering (elastic, wavelength-dependent), and Raman scattering (inelastic, molecular transitions).

*Energy flow:* Partial and bidirectional. In Compton scattering, energy is transferred from photon to electron (or vice versa for inverse Compton). In elastic scattering, no net energy transfer occurs but the photon's momentum direction changes.

*Information flow:* Scattering modifies the photon's information content — it can depolarize, redirect, or frequency-shift the photon, encoding information about the scatterer into the outgoing photon state. This is simultaneously an information-erasure operation (scrambling the original encoding) and an information-writing operation (encoding scatterer properties).

**N.4 — Measurement / Wave-Particle Duality Interface**
The boundary crossing where the photon transitions from a delocalized quantum superposition (wave-like propagation through multiple paths) to a localized detection event (particle-like "click" at a specific detector). This is the quantum measurement interface.

*Energy flow:* Prior to measurement, the photon's energy is distributed across the probability amplitude of its wavefunction. Upon detection, the full quantum of energy E = hν is delivered to a single spatial location. The energy itself does not transform in kind but undergoes a dramatic change in localization — from everywhere consistent with the wavefunction to one specific point.

*Information flow:* This interface destroys the photon's superposition information (which-path quantum information is collapsed) while producing classical information (a detection record at a specific spacetime location). It is a one-way gate from quantum to classical information — the quintessential decoherence interface corresponding to N.1 in the reference file's semiclassical framework.

**N.5 — Vacuum-Medium Interface**
The boundary crossing where the photon transitions from vacuum propagation (v = c, no dispersion) to propagation within a material medium (v = c/n, dispersion relation modified). At a dielectric surface, the photon's effective properties change discontinuously — phase velocity, group velocity, and polarization behavior all transform.

*Energy flow:* The photon's energy (frequency) is conserved across this interface, but its wavelength and momentum change. Inside the medium, the photon's energy is shared between the electromagnetic field and the medium's polarization response (polariton picture). Upon exit back to vacuum, the original vacuum properties are restored.

*Information flow:* Polarization information may be rotated (birefringent media), phase information is accumulated differently (optical path length changes), and spectral components may separate (dispersion). This interface provides the physics underlying lenses, prisms, optical fibers, and all refractive optics — devices that process photon information by exploiting the vacuum-medium boundary.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These five interfaces are unambiguously distinct. They mediate between different regime pairs (matter/radiation, wave/particle, vacuum/medium), employ different physical mechanisms (quantum transition rules, conservation constraints, decoherence, dielectric response), and can be independently engaged or bypassed. A photon in intergalactic vacuum experiences none of N.3 or N.5 but is still subject to N.4 upon eventual detection. The interfaces project to different physics: N.1 and N.2 are governed by atomic/molecular transition theory; N.3 by scattering cross-section calculations; N.4 by decoherence and measurement theory; N.5 by condensed-matter electrodynamics. Each constitutes a genuinely independent subsystem.

### Element 7: ENVIRONMENT (E) — 4 Subsystems Identified

The photon's environment decomposes into four hierarchically embedded contexts, each providing different resource types, constraints, and varieties of perturbation. Close examination reveals distinct energy and information flows between the photon and each environmental layer.

**E.1 — QED Vacuum**

**E.2 — Gravitational Field / Spacetime Curvature**

*Energy flows:* Gravitational redshift and blueshift transform photon energy as it climbs out of or falls into gravitational potential wells (Δν/ν = ΔΦ/c²). *Information flows:* Spacetime curvature imposes information about the mass-energy distribution of the universe onto photon trajectories. **E.3 — Material Media**

*Information flows:* The medium imposes its structural information onto the photon through spectral absorption lines (encoding chemical composition), Faraday rotation (encoding magnetic field strength), and scattering patterns (encoding particle size distributions). **E.4 — Cosmological Context**

*Energy flows:* Cosmic expansion continuously redshifts all photons, draining energy from radiation into the expansion of space itself. The cosmic microwave background (CMB) represents a relic photon population in thermal equilibrium with the cosmological environment at T ≈ 2.725 K. *Information flows:* The CMB carries information about the state of the universe at recombination (z ≈ 1100) — temperature fluctuations, polarization patterns (E-mode and B-mode), and spectral distortions encode primordial density perturbations, baryon-photon coupling, and potentially primordial gravitational waves. Photons are the primary carriers of cosmological information across the observable universe.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These four environmental contexts provide qualitatively different resources and impose different constraint types. E.1 operates at quantum-field scales, E.2 at gravitational scales, E.3 at atomic/molecular scales, and E.4 at cosmological scales.

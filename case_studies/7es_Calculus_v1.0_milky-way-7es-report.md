## DETAILED ANALYSIS BY ELEMENT

### Element 1: INPUT (I) — 5 Subsystems Identified

Input defines where energy-information enters the Milky Way system from its environment. Five distinct input subsystems are identified, each operating through fundamentally different physical mechanisms.

**I.1 — Primordial and Recycled Gas Accretion**
Hydrogen and helium flow into the galaxy from the intergalactic medium along cosmic filaments. Cold-mode accretion channels gas directly onto the disk through narrow streams, while hot-mode accretion involves gas cooling from the circumgalactic halo. This is the primary mass input sustaining ongoing star formation. The mechanism is gravitational capture and radiative cooling of baryonic matter — a thermodynamic and hydrodynamic process fundamentally distinct from the collisionless dynamics of dark matter.

**I.2 — Dark Matter Halo Accretion**
The galaxy's dark matter halo grows through the continuous merger and accretion of smaller dark matter subhalos. This input is collisionless gravitational aggregation, operating through an entirely different mechanism from baryonic gas accretion. Dark matter interacts only gravitationally, producing no electromagnetic signature. It enters the system through hierarchical structure formation (bottom-up merging of smaller halos), reshaping the gravitational potential well that governs all baryonic dynamics.

**I.3 — Satellite Galaxy and Merger Inputs**
Dwarf galaxies and larger satellite systems (e.g., the Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal, the ancient Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage merger roughly 8–10 billion years ago) deliver composite packages of stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and pre-enriched metals. This is qualitatively distinct from smooth gas or dark matter accretion: it introduces pre-processed, structured material with its own dynamical history, chemical composition, and stellar populations. The mechanism is tidal capture and gravitational disruption.

**I.4 — Radiation and Energetic Particle Inputs**
The galaxy receives the cosmic microwave background radiation (redshifted thermal spectrum from the last scattering surface), the extragalactic ultraviolet background (from quasars and star-forming galaxies), and extragalactic cosmic rays (ultra-high-energy protons and nuclei accelerated in distant astrophysical sources). These electromagnetic and particle inputs are fundamentally different in mechanism from gravitational mass accretion, operating through photon absorption and charged-particle interactions with the galactic magnetic field.

**I.5 — Angular Momentum and Tidal Torque Inputs**
During formation, tidal torques from neighboring protogalactic structures and the cosmic web imparted angular momentum to the proto-Milky Way. This is a conserved dynamical quantity that determined the galaxy's disk geometry and rotation. The mechanism is gravitational torque rather than mass transfer — a distinct physical quantity entering the system that shaped its morphological evolution.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These five subsystems are genuine, not artificial divisions of a single process. They utilize fundamentally different physical mechanisms (hydrodynamic cooling vs. collisionless gravitational aggregation vs. tidal disruption vs. electromagnetic absorption vs. gravitational torque), respond to different stimuli (gas density vs. halo mass function vs. satellite orbital parameters vs. photon flux vs. tidal field), and project to partially segregated processing centers (disk accretion vs. halo growth vs. stellar stream formation vs. interstellar medium heating vs. disk angular momentum budget). Loss of any single channel would not eliminate the others.

### Element 2: OUTPUT (O) — 5 Subsystems Identified

Output defines where energy-information exits the Milky Way system boundary, crossing into the circumgalactic medium, intergalactic medium, or neighboring systems.

**O.1 — Electromagnetic Radiation Emission**
The galaxy radiates across the full electromagnetic spectrum: radio synchrotron from relativistic electrons spiraling in magnetic fields, infrared thermal emission from dust-reprocessed starlight, optical and ultraviolet emission from stellar photospheres, X-ray emission from hot gas and compact object accretion, and gamma-ray emission from cosmic ray interactions and compact sources. This is the primary observable output and the mechanism through which the galaxy dissipates processed energy into its environment. Each spectral regime traces different physical processes, but all share the common mechanism of photon emission.

**O.2 — Galactic Winds and Mass Outflows**
Supernova-driven outflows and (historically) AGN-driven jets expel chemically enriched gas from the disk into the circumgalactic medium and beyond. These outflows carry kinetic energy, thermal energy, and processed metals out of the galaxy, representing a mass-energy output fundamentally distinct from photon emission. The mechanism is hydrodynamic and magnetohydrodynamic momentum transfer from explosive events. Evidence includes the Fermi Bubbles — large-scale gamma-ray and X-ray structures extending above and below the galactic plane, likely driven by past Sgr A* activity or concentrated star formation.

**O.3 — Gravitational Influence on Surroundings**
The galaxy exerts tidal forces on satellite galaxies (stripping the Sagittarius Dwarf, disrupting the Magellanic Clouds), contributes to dynamical friction within the Local Group, and generates gravitational lensing effects on background light. This is an output operating through spacetime curvature rather than particle or photon emission — a fundamentally different output mechanism.

**O.4 — Stellar Streams and Ejected Stars**
Tidal disruption of satellite galaxies produces stellar streams (e.g., the Sagittarius stream), while dynamical interactions near Sgr A* eject hypervelocity stars at speeds exceeding galactic escape velocity. These are material outputs — organized structures of mass leaving or redistributing within the system boundary — distinct from diffuse winds or radiation.

**O.5 — Cosmic Ray Output**
Supernova remnants, pulsars, and other energetic sites accelerate charged particles to relativistic energies. A fraction of these cosmic rays escape the galactic magnetic confinement and enter the intergalactic medium. This is an energetic particle output operating through electromagnetic acceleration mechanisms distinct from thermal gas outflows or photon radiation.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These five outputs employ different physical mechanisms (photon emission vs. bulk hydrodynamic flow vs. spacetime curvature vs. tidal mass redistribution vs. particle acceleration), target different environmental components (radiation field vs. CGM gas vs. satellite dynamics vs. halo stellar populations vs. IGM particle background), and can operate independently (the galaxy radiates even without active outflows; gravitational influence persists regardless of electromagnetic output).

### Element 3: PROCESSING (P) — 6 Subsystems Identified

Processing defines where energy-information undergoes transformation within the galaxy, changing form or organization while remaining within the system boundary. The Milky Way exhibits six distinct processing subsystems.

**P.1 — Star Formation**
The conversion of diffuse interstellar gas into gravitationally bound stellar objects through the Jeans instability, molecular cloud collapse, protostellar accretion, and main-sequence ignition. This is the galaxy's primary transformation engine, converting gravitational potential energy and gas-phase chemical potential into luminous stellar output. The mechanism is gravitational-hydrodynamic: gas cools, fragments, collapses, and ignites nuclear fusion. Star formation occurs predominantly in the disk's spiral arms, the central molecular zone, and occasionally in tidal interaction regions.

**P.2 — Stellar Nucleosynthesis**
Within stellar interiors, nuclear fusion transforms light elements into heavier ones through multiple distinct pathways: the proton-proton chain and CNO cycle in hydrogen burning, the triple-alpha process in helium burning, carbon and oxygen burning in massive stars, silicon burning approaching iron-peak elements, the s-process (slow neutron capture in AGB stars), and the r-process (rapid neutron capture in neutron star mergers and core-collapse supernovae). This is nuclear-physical transformation, fundamentally different from the gravitational-hydrodynamic mechanism of star formation. It operates within stars rather than in the interstellar medium.

**P.3 — Gravitational Dynamics and Structural Processing**
N-body gravitational interactions organize matter into coherent large-scale structures: spiral arms arise from density wave propagation, the central bar forms through dynamical instability, the bulge builds through secular evolution and merger remnants, and the stellar halo assembles from disrupted satellite debris. The mechanism is collisionless gravitational dynamics — orbital mechanics, resonances, dynamical friction, and phase-space mixing. This is a distinct transformation from either star formation or nucleosynthesis, operating on the organizational structure of the galaxy rather than on the physical state of individual gas parcels or nuclei.

**P.4 — Chemical Evolution of the Interstellar Medium**
The progressive enrichment of the ISM through the cycling of stellar death products (supernovae ejecta, AGB winds, planetary nebulae, neutron star merger debris) back into the gas reservoir. Each stellar generation processes the ISM to higher metallicity, altering cooling functions, dust content, and subsequent star formation characteristics. This is a galactic-scale chemical processing loop distinct from nucleosynthesis within individual stars — it operates on the bulk ISM composition through mixing, dilution, and re-incorporation.

**P.5 — Central Black Hole (Sgr A*) Processing**
The supermassive black hole at the galactic center (approximately 4 million solar masses) processes infalling matter through gravitational accretion, converting rest-mass energy to radiation and kinetic outflows with efficiencies up to 10–40% (far exceeding nuclear fusion at roughly 0.7%). Though currently in a low-luminosity state, Sgr A* was likely more active in the past, and its processing fundamentally shaped the central galactic environment. The mechanism is general-relativistic accretion — qualitatively different from any other processing pathway.

**P.6 — Interstellar Medium Phase Processing**
Gas in the ISM cycles through multiple phases — cold molecular clouds (10–20 K), cold atomic gas (50–100 K), warm neutral and ionized medium (6,000–10,000 K), and hot ionized gas (10⁶–10⁷ K) — driven by supernova shocks, stellar radiation, cosmic ray heating, and radiative cooling. This thermodynamic phase cycling is a continuous transformation process distinct from star formation (which consumes gas) or chemical evolution (which changes composition). It governs the state and distribution of the raw material available for all other processing.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These six processing subsystems operate through different physical mechanisms (gravitational collapse vs. nuclear fusion vs. orbital mechanics vs. chemical mixing vs. relativistic accretion vs. thermodynamic phase transitions), at different spatial scales (molecular cloud cores vs. stellar interiors vs. galactic disk vs. ISM volume vs. central parsecs vs. multi-phase ISM), and on different timescales (millions of years for star formation vs. billions of years for nucleosynthetic enrichment cycles vs. continuous for gravitational dynamics). Each can be analyzed independently, and the loss of any single subsystem would not eliminate the others (a galaxy without a central black hole still forms stars; a galaxy without spiral arms still processes elements).

### Element 4: CONTROLS (C) — 6 Subsystems Identified

Controls define where energy-information flows are constrained, directed, or channeled according to predetermined parameters. Controls are proactive — they define what states and behaviors are permissible before flow occurs.

**C.1 — Fundamental Physical Constants and Laws**
The gravitational constant G, speed of light c, Planck's constant ℏ, electromagnetic coupling α, and nuclear force parameters constrain all galactic processes at the most basic level. These are non-negotiable structural constraints embedded in the fabric of physics itself. They determine stellar lifetimes, nuclear reaction rates, gravitational binding energies, and photon interaction cross-sections. No galactic process can violate these constraints.

**C.2 — Dark Matter Halo Gravitational Potential**
The dark matter halo establishes the gravitational potential well within which all baryonic dynamics unfold. It constrains the galaxy's rotation curve (maintaining flat rotation at approximately 220 km/s beyond the solar radius), determines the virial radius (roughly 200 kpc), governs satellite orbital dynamics, and sets the escape velocity. This is a distinct control from fundamental constants — it is a structural constraint specific to this particular galaxy, shaped by its formation history. The halo potential is established before baryonic processing begins and constrains all subsequent flows.

**C.3 — Angular Momentum Conservation**
The total angular momentum of the proto-galactic gas cloud, acquired from tidal torques during formation, constrains the galaxy's morphology: matter cannot collapse to arbitrarily small radii without shedding angular momentum, which forces the formation of a rotating disk. This conservation law governs disk scale height, spiral arm geometry, bar instability thresholds, and the radial distribution of star formation. It is a constraint distinct from the gravitational potential (which sets depth) by governing rotational geometry.

**C.4 — Jeans Mass and Gravitational Instability Thresholds**
The Jeans mass M_J ∝ T^(3/2) ρ^(-1/2) sets the minimum mass for gravitational collapse in a gas cloud, determining which regions of the ISM can form stars and which remain stable. The Toomre stability criterion Q = (c_s κ)/(π G Σ) controls disk-scale instabilities. These are threshold controls — binary gates that permit or prohibit star formation based on local thermodynamic conditions. They are distinct from the gravitational potential (which is a global constraint) by operating as local conditional switches.

**C.5 — Metallicity and Dust Opacity Controls**
The metallicity of the ISM controls cooling rates (metal-line cooling dominates below 10⁴ K), which in turn controls the Jeans mass and thus the initial mass function of newly formed stars. Dust opacity controls radiative transfer, shielding molecular cloud cores from UV dissociation. These are chemical-state constraints that modulate processing efficiency — distinct from gravitational or dynamical constraints by operating through atomic and molecular physics.

**C.6 — Galactic Magnetic Field Constraints**
The large-scale galactic magnetic field (approximately 5–10 microgauss in the disk) provides non-thermal pressure support against gravitational collapse, channels cosmic ray propagation, mediates angular momentum transport through magnetorotational instability, and influences the morphology of supernova remnants and outflows. This is an electromagnetic constraint operating through an entirely different force from gravitational or thermodynamic controls.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These six control subsystems enforce different types of constraints (universal physical laws vs. particular gravitational architecture vs. conserved dynamical quantity vs. local thermodynamic thresholds vs. chemical-state modulation vs. electromagnetic pressure), operate at different scales (universal vs. galaxy-wide vs. global rotational vs. local cloud-scale vs. molecular-scale vs. magnetohydrodynamic), and involve different physical mechanisms. The dark matter potential is set before baryonic collapse; angular momentum is conserved from formation; Jeans criteria operate locally in real time; metallicity evolves over galactic time; magnetic fields thread the disk continuously.

### Element 5: FEEDBACK (F) — 8 Subsystems Identified (4 Active + 4 Passive)

Feedback defines where energy-information returns to confirm, regulate, or challenge system viability. The Milky Way exhibits both active and passive feedback modes, confirming the framework's universal dual-mode pattern.

**ACTIVE FEEDBACK**

**F.1 — Supernova Feedback**
Core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae inject approximately 10⁵¹ ergs of kinetic energy per event into the surrounding ISM. This disrupts molecular clouds, heats ambient gas to 10⁶–10⁷ K, drives turbulence that supports against further collapse, and can trigger star formation in adjacent compressed shells. This is the galaxy's primary self-regulation mechanism: star formation produces massive stars that explode, which disrupts the conditions for further star formation, creating a negative feedback loop. The correction signal is proportional to the star formation rate — higher star formation produces more supernovae, which more strongly suppress subsequent star formation.

**F.2 — AGN Feedback from Sgr A***
The central supermassive black hole provides feedback through two modes: radiative (quasar-mode, when accretion rates are high, producing intense radiation pressure that heats and expels gas) and mechanical (jet/wind-mode, producing kinetic outflows that inflate cavities in the surrounding medium). Though Sgr A* is currently in a low-luminosity state, evidence from the Fermi Bubbles and X-ray observations suggests recent periods of greater activity. This is a distinct feedback mechanism from supernovae — it operates from a single central source through relativistic accretion physics, while supernova feedback is distributed throughout the star-forming disk.

**F.3 — Stellar Wind Feedback**
Massive O and B stars drive powerful line-driven winds (mass loss rates of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ solar masses per year) that create wind-blown bubbles, compress surrounding gas, and return processed material to the ISM before the star explodes as a supernova. AGB stars shed their envelopes more gently, returning s-process elements and carbon. This is distinct from supernova feedback in timescale (continuous during stellar lifetime vs. instantaneous explosion), energy injection mechanism (momentum-driven wind vs. energy-driven blast wave), and chemical yield (wind composition differs from explosive nucleosynthesis products).

**F.4 — Photoionization and Radiation Pressure Feedback**
Ultraviolet radiation from young massive stars ionizes surrounding hydrogen, creating HII regions that expand and disperse the parent molecular cloud. Radiation pressure on dust grains provides additional momentum coupling. This operates on shorter timescales than supernova feedback (beginning immediately upon star formation rather than after millions of years of stellar evolution) and through a different physical mechanism (photon-matter interaction rather than blast wave propagation). It is the earliest-acting feedback channel, disrupting star-forming clouds before the most massive stars reach the supernova stage.

**PASSIVE FEEDBACK**

**F.5 — Structural Persistence of the Disk-Bulge-Halo Architecture**
The Milky Way has maintained a coherent rotating disk, central bulge, and extended stellar halo for over 10 billion years, surviving the Gaia-Enceladus merger event, continuous satellite accretion, and secular dynamical evolution. This persistent structural coherence is not maintained by any explicit signaling loop — it is the continuous existential confirmation that the galaxy's internal variety (gravitational binding, angular momentum support, ongoing star formation) adequately matches environmental perturbation (tidal forces, gas stripping, merger disruption). The galaxy's continued existence as a recognizable barred spiral IS the feedback.

**F.6 — Dark Matter Halo Stability**
The dark matter halo has maintained a stable gravitational potential well since early formation, supporting the virial equilibrium of the entire system. No explicit monitoring or correction signal maintains this stability — it persists through the collisionless relaxation of the dark matter particle distribution in phase space. The halo's continued structural integrity confirms that gravitational binding energy exceeds kinetic disruption energy.

**F.7 — Sustained Star Formation Rate**
The Milky Way has maintained a star formation rate of approximately 1–3 solar masses per year for billions of years, neither exhausting its gas supply in a rapid burst nor quenching entirely. This steady-state persistence indicates that the self-regulation between gas accretion (Input), star formation (Processing), and stellar feedback (active feedback) has achieved a dynamical equilibrium. The continued presence of star-forming molecular clouds is passive confirmation that the gas cycling system remains viable.

**F.8 — Chemical Enrichment Floor**
The ISM metallicity has been maintained above the threshold necessary for efficient gas cooling and continued star formation. The minimum metallicity never dropped below levels that would prevent molecular cloud formation after initial Population III enrichment. This persistent chemical state is passive confirmation that nucleosynthetic processing and ISM mixing have maintained viable conditions for ongoing galactic evolution.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** The four active feedback subsystems operate through different physical mechanisms (blast waves vs. relativistic accretion vs. stellar winds vs. photoionization), at different spatial scales (distributed disk-wide vs. central source vs. individual stellar neighborhoods vs. HII region scale), and on different timescales (post-main-sequence vs. accretion-rate-dependent vs. continuous during stellar lifetime vs. immediate upon star formation). The four passive feedback subsystems confirm viability at different organizational levels (global morphology vs. gravitational potential vs. star formation ecology vs. chemical state). All eight subsystems are functionally independent — each can be disrupted without immediately eliminating the others.

### Element 6: INTERFACE (N) — 6 Subsystems Identified

Interface defines where energy-information crosses boundaries between incompatible regimes, requiring translation, transduction, or mediation. The Milky Way exhibits six distinct interface subsystems — a particularly rich set that reflects the galaxy's position at the intersection of multiple physical regimes.

**N.1 — Circumgalactic Medium (CGM) Interface**
The CGM is the extended gaseous halo (extending to approximately the virial radius of 200 kpc) that mediates all exchanges between the galaxy's disk and the intergalactic medium. Cold gas streams from the cosmic web must penetrate and interact with the hot CGM (at virial temperature approximately 10⁶ K) before reaching the disk. Outflows driven by supernovae and AGN activity must traverse the CGM before escaping to the IGM. The CGM acts as a buffer, cooling flow regulator, and recycling reservoir — gas expelled by feedback can cool and re-accrete rather than being permanently lost. This interface mediates between the hot, diffuse, pressure-supported halo regime and the cold, dense, rotationally-supported disk regime. It is the galaxy's most critical boundary, analogous to the semiclassical interface (N.3) in the quantum field theory formulation: both mediate transitions between fundamentally incompatible operational regimes.

**N.2 — Disk-Halo Interface**
The boundary between the thin star-forming disk (scale height approximately 300 pc for the thin disk) and the extended hot gaseous and stellar halo. Supernova-driven superbubbles breach this boundary, venting hot gas into the halo in chimney structures. Galactic fountain flows carry enriched gas upward, which cools and re-accretes onto the disk. High-velocity clouds fall through this interface, delivering fresh gas. This boundary mediates between the rotationally-supported, high-density, magnetically-threaded disk regime and the quasi-spherical, low-density, pressure-supported halo regime.

**N.3 — Spiral Arm Density Wave Interface**
Spiral arms are not material structures but density waves propagating through the disk. Gas and stars orbit the galaxy at different velocities than the spiral pattern speed. As gas enters a spiral arm, it encounters a convergent flow that compresses it, potentially triggering gravitational instability and star formation. As it exits, the compressed gas may expand and disperse. The spiral arm interface mediates between the inter-arm regime (diffuse, stable, low star formation rate) and the intra-arm regime (compressed, unstable, high star formation rate). This is a moving interface — a propagating boundary that continuously sweeps through the disk, periodically transforming the state of gas parcels that encounter it.

**N.4 — Star-ISM Interface (Stellar Boundary)**
Individual stars interact with the interstellar medium through a complex boundary zone: stellar winds create wind-blown bubbles, UV radiation creates ionized HII regions, supernovae create expanding blast waves, and AGB stars shed dusty envelopes. This interface mediates the transition between the stellar regime (nuclear-physical, gravitationally bound, high-temperature plasma) and the ISM regime (hydrodynamic, magnetically influenced, multi-phase). It is the primary site of chemical enrichment transfer — where nucleosynthetic products cross from stellar interiors into the galactic gas reservoir.

**N.5 — Galactic Center Interface**
The central few hundred parsecs constitute a distinct interface zone between the main galactic disk and the supermassive black hole environment. The Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) contains approximately 10% of the galaxy's molecular gas concentrated in roughly 0.1% of its area, with extreme turbulence, magnetic field strengths, and tidal shear. The nuclear star cluster and circumnuclear disk mediate matter transport toward Sgr A*. This interface translates between the disk regime (ordered rotation, moderate densities) and the black hole regime (relativistic dynamics, extreme tidal forces, potential jet launching).

**N.6 — Satellite Galaxy Tidal Interface**
The tidal boundary around the Milky Way (defined by the Roche lobe for each satellite) mediates the transition of material from satellite galaxy membership to Milky Way membership. As satellite galaxies orbit within the Milky Way's dark matter halo, tidal forces progressively strip their outer stars, gas, and dark matter. The tidal radius defines the boundary where gravitational loyalty shifts from satellite to host. This interface mediates between two distinct gravitational systems, translating bound satellite material into free-streaming tidal debris (stellar streams) that phase-mixes into the Milky Way's halo.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These six interfaces mediate between different pairs of incompatible regimes (hot halo / cold disk, disk / halo, inter-arm / intra-arm, stellar interior / ISM, disk / black hole, satellite / host), employ different physical mechanisms (radiative cooling and shock mediation, buoyant venting and fountain flows, density wave compression, wind and radiation interaction, extreme tidal and magnetic mediation, gravitational stripping), and operate at fundamentally different spatial scales (200 kpc for CGM, 300 pc for disk-halo, kiloparsecs for spiral arms, parsecs for stellar boundaries, parsecs to hundreds of parsecs for the galactic center, tens of kpc for tidal stripping). Each interface can be studied independently, and each mediates a physically distinct transition.

### Element 7: ENVIRONMENT (E) — 5 Subsystems Identified

Environment defines where energy-information is sourced from and dissipated to, providing the thermodynamic context and resource base for the galaxy's operations.

**E.1 — The Local Group**
The Milky Way is gravitationally bound within the Local Group, a system of approximately 80+ galaxies dominated by the Milky Way and Andromeda (M31), separated by roughly 770 kpc and approaching at approximately 110 km/s. The Local Group provides the immediate gravitational environment: tidal interactions with M31 influence the outer halo and satellite population, the Magellanic Clouds (Large and Small) are the nearest major satellite interaction partners, and the Local Group barycenter defines the galaxy's large-scale trajectory. This is the most proximate environmental context, operating through direct gravitational interaction on timescales of billions of years.

**E.2 — The Cosmic Web and Large-Scale Structure**
The Milky Way sits within the Laniakea Supercluster, embedded in the filamentary cosmic web of dark matter and baryonic gas. Cosmic filaments channel cold gas accretion toward the galaxy, while large-scale gravitational flows (the Milky Way participates in the bulk flow toward the Great Attractor region) modulate the galaxy's trajectory and accretion environment. This is a distinct environmental context from the Local Group: it operates on scales of tens to hundreds of megaparsecs and influences the galaxy through large-scale density gradients and filamentary gas supply rather than direct gravitational binding.

**E.3 — The Cosmological Context**
The expanding universe, governed by the Friedmann equations and driven by dark energy, provides the ultimate boundary condition for galactic evolution. The Hubble parameter H(t) determines the rate at which the cosmic web stretches, affecting accretion rates and merger frequencies over cosmic time. The cosmic microwave background provides a uniform radiation bath. The cosmological parameters (Ωm, ΩΛ, H₀) were established in the early universe and constrain the entire subsequent history of structure formation. This is distinct from local structure by operating at the scale of the observable universe and through the global metric expansion of space.

**E.4 — The Intergalactic Medium (IGM)**
The diffuse gas between galaxies (overdensities and underdensities of baryonic matter in the cosmic web) provides the reservoir from which the galaxy ultimately draws its baryonic fuel and into which it expels processed outflows. The IGM has its own temperature, density, and metallicity structure shaped by the UV background, galaxy feedback, and large-scale structure formation. This is distinct from the cosmic web (E.2) by being specifically the baryonic gas component rather than the dark matter scaffolding, and it operates as both a source (accretion) and sink (outflow absorption) for the galaxy.

**E.5 — The Extragalactic Radiation and Particle Environment**
The galaxy is bathed in the extragalactic UV background (from quasars and star-forming galaxies at all redshifts), the cosmic neutrino background, and the flux of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays from distant astrophysical sources. This radiation and particle environment sets the ionization state of the IGM and CGM, influences the thermal state of accreting gas, and provides a continuous particle input. It is distinct from the other environmental subsystems by operating through electromagnetic and particle physics rather than gravitational dynamics.

**Subsystem Distinctness Assessment:** These five environmental subsystems provide different resource types and constraint categories (gravitational binding and tidal interaction vs. filamentary gas supply vs. metric expansion and cosmological parameters vs. baryonic reservoir vs. radiation/particle flux), operate at different spatial scales (approximately 1 Mpc for Local Group, approximately 10–100 Mpc for cosmic web, cosmic scale for cosmological context, variable for IGM, cosmic for radiation background), and contribute different varieties of perturbation (tidal disruption, accretion modulation, expansion rate evolution, gas supply and sink, ionization and heating).
